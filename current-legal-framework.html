Overview

China does not currently have a single national law that generally prohibits cruelty toward all animals. In a 2019 official response to a National People's Congress proposal, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs stated that China had not enacted a unified anti-cruelty law, while noting that animal-related protections appear across several laws, regulations, rules, and policy documents.

The existing framework is therefore divided by subject. Different rules apply depending on whether an animal is treated as wildlife, livestock, an animal involved in disease-control measures, a laboratory animal, an exhibited animal, or private property.

These laws can regulate matters such as quarantine, transport, infectious disease, breeding, slaughter, wildlife trafficking, public health, and emergency response. They do not create a comprehensive welfare system for ordinary companion animals such as pet dogs and cats.

Scope of this page

This page provides general background information and links to primary legal sources. It is not legal advice and does not claim that every animal-related incident falls within the laws summarized below.

What the Framework Covers

Area Main Focus Typical Limitation
Animal disease prevention Disease monitoring, quarantine, inspection, treatment, disposal, and public-health protection. Primarily concerned with disease control rather than general protection from cruelty.
Wildlife protection Protected species, habitats, hunting, breeding, transportation, trade, and illegal consumption. Applies to wildlife categories defined by law, not generally to domestic dogs and cats.
Livestock regulation Breeding, husbandry, production, transport, slaughter, genetic resources, food supply, and public-health risks. Applies to animals listed within the national livestock and poultry genetic-resources system.
Emergency animal-epidemic response Government authority, quarantine zones, emergency control, culling, disinfection, and compensation. Triggered by qualifying animal epidemics and is not a general companion-animal welfare law.

Principal Laws and Regulations

Disease Prevention

Animal Epidemic Prevention Law

Governs the prevention, control, diagnosis, elimination, and quarantine of animal diseases, as well as the safe disposal of dead animals and animal products that may present disease risks.

The law defines “animals” for its purposes as livestock, poultry, and other animals that are artificially raised or captured. Its central purpose is disease control, public health, and protection of the animal-production sector.

Wildlife

Wildlife Protection Law

Establishes protection and management systems for legally defined wildlife and their habitats. It regulates hunting, breeding, sale, purchase, use, transport, and trade.

The 2022 revision states that artificially bred wildlife must be kept under conditions suited to the animals' habits and must not be abused. It also prohibits specified forms of illegal hunting, transport, trade, and consumption.

Revised in 2022 · Effective 1 May 2023

Livestock

Animal Husbandry Law

Regulates livestock and poultry genetic resources, breeding, raising, commercial operation, transport, and slaughter. Its purposes include product supply and safety, public-health risk prevention, and development of the livestock sector.

Its scope is tied to animals included in the national livestock and poultry genetic-resources catalogue. Dogs and cats are not treated as ordinary livestock under that catalogue.

Revised in 2022 · Effective 1 March 2023

Emergency Response

Regulation on Emergency Responses to Major Animal Epidemics

Sets out government powers and procedures for responding to serious animal epidemics, including reporting, emergency command structures, movement restrictions, quarantine, culling, disinfection, disposal, and support measures.

It is especially relevant when authorities describe an animal-removal operation as an epidemic-control measure, but it does not itself provide a general legal basis for cruelty prevention.

Promulgated in 2005 · Amended in 2017

Companion Animals

Dogs and cats may be affected by rules concerning disease prevention, quarantine, transport, ownership, property damage, public order, food safety, and local dog-management systems. However, those rules do not amount to a unified national companion-animal welfare code.

In its 2019 response to a proposal for an anti-cruelty law, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs expressly stated that a unified anti-cruelty law had not been enacted. The response instead identified separate protections relating to livestock, wildlife, laboratory animals, zoo animals, and other regulated contexts.

This distinction is important. The absence of a comprehensive anti-cruelty statute does not mean that every harmful act is automatically lawful. Depending on the facts, existing laws concerning property, theft, public order, illegal business activity, wildlife, food safety, disease control, or online conduct may still apply.

Local governments may also maintain rules on dog licensing, rabies vaccination, leashing, restricted breeds, public-space management, and the handling of unowned animals. These rules differ by city and should be examined separately from national law.

Relevance to Archived Cases

Epidemic-control removals

Cases such as the Shangrao corgi and Snowball incidents require close examination of the legal authority claimed by local officials, the existence of a confirmed epidemic, applicable quarantine or emergency plans, and whether the response was proportionate and procedurally documented.

Cat theft and transport

Large cat-transport cases may involve several distinct legal questions: whether particular cats were stolen property, whether transport and quarantine documents were required, whether protected wildlife was used as bait, whether business or food-safety rules were violated, and whether police or agricultural authorities had grounds to intervene.

Deliberate cruelty toward companion animals

Where a case concerns deliberate harm to an owned or community dog or cat, the central legal gap is that China does not have a single national anti-cruelty offence applying generally to all companion animals. Other legal routes may exist, but they depend heavily on ownership, public impact, evidence, location, and the specific conduct involved.
