Case Summary

On 13 and 14 September 2022, eleven companion dogs were poisoned in a residential community in Beijing's Chaoyang District. Nine dogs died and two survived. Several stray cats were also reported to have been poisoned during the same period.

Papi, a 13-year-old West Highland White Terrier, was among the dogs that died after consuming poisoned bait. Police investigations later identified a 65-year-old resident surnamed Zhang as the suspect responsible for placing poisoned food throughout the community.

The case developed into one of the most closely followed criminal proceedings involving companion animals in Beijing. It also became a focal point for discussions on animal protection, civil compensation, and legal reform in China.

Background

According to published reporting, residents began finding dogs suffering sudden symptoms of poisoning on the evening of 13 September and the following morning.

Investigators later determined that chopped chicken bones had been soaked in sodium fluoroacetate (also reported as fluoroacetic acid) and deliberately scattered throughout common areas of the residential compound, including areas close to where children regularly played.

Papi's owner joined other affected families in pursuing criminal accountability while also advocating for stronger legal protections for companion animals. The case remained active for several years before a judgment was finally delivered.

Legal Proceedings

Because China does not currently have a comprehensive national anti-cruelty law protecting companion animals, prosecutors relied on existing criminal provisions concerning the use and spreading of dangerous substances that endanger public safety.

According to court reporting, poisoned chicken bones containing sodium fluoroacetate were deliberately placed throughout public areas of the residential compound. The toxic bait posed a risk not only to companion animals, but also to children and other residents using shared community spaces.

Zhang admitted to placing the poisoned bait during court proceedings. He was sentenced to four years' imprisonment. Later reporting stated that he immediately appealed the judgment after sentencing.

The case has been widely described as Beijing's first criminal public prosecution involving the deliberate poisoning of companion animals.

Public Response

Public attention

The deaths of Papi and the other companion animals attracted widespread discussion among pet owners, lawyers, veterinarians, and animal-protection advocates throughout China.

Many observers closely followed the legal proceedings, particularly because the criminal trial lasted several years before a judgment was issued.

Legal significance

The case demonstrated that existing public-safety laws may be applied when deliberate poisoning threatens both companion animals and members of the public. Nevertheless, many legal scholars and advocates argued that China still lacks comprehensive legislation that directly addresses cruelty toward companion animals.

Continuing influence

Papi's case continues to be cited during discussions concerning companion-animal welfare, civil damages, criminal accountability, and future animal-protection legislation in China.

Verification Notes

This page is based on publicly available media reporting and secondary reference materials. Voices For Animals CN has not independently investigated the scene, reviewed the complete court file, or interviewed every party involved.

Sixth Tone is used primarily for detailed background about Papi, the poisoning, the affected owners, and the legal proceedings through October 2024. Channel NewsAsia is used for the later report that Zhang received a four-year prison sentence and filed an immediate appeal.

Baidu Baike is included as a Chinese-language secondary overview and timeline. It is not an official court record. Where accounts differ, this archive gives greater weight to court documents, official notices, and established news reporting.

English-language reports use slightly different translations of the criminal offence, including “using and spreading dangerous substances.” This page uses that wording as a readable translation and does not present it as the official English text of the Chinese judgment.
